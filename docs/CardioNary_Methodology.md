# CardioNary — Methodology

CardioNary was developed as two separate but connected screening pipelines: one for chest X-ray images to detect pneumonia and another for heart sounds to identify abnormal cardiac activity. Each pipeline uses a combination of a deep learning model, additional medical features, and patient information to produce predictions. These predictions are later combined to generate a single screening result for each patient visit.

**Chest X-ray Analysis:** The X-ray images were first converted into grayscale, resized to 96 × 96 pixels, and enhanced to improve the visibility of important lung regions. These images were then processed using a MobileNetV2-based model, initially trained on the pediatric Kermany dataset and evaluated on the adult RSNA pneumonia dataset to study its performance on a different age group. Alongside the main model, additional image characteristics such as contrast, texture, and the affected lung region were used by a separate logistic regression model. Patient information, including age and sex, was also considered through another classifier. However, an error in processing the RSNA age values resulted in missing age information, which affected the evaluation of this part of the system.

**Heart-Sound Analysis:** Heart recordings were divided into three-second segments and converted into spectrograms, which represent how sound frequencies change over time. These were used as inputs to a compact CNN trained on the Yaseen heart-sound dataset. A sound-quality check was also introduced to identify recordings that were unsuitable for reliable analysis. In addition, features such as heart rate, rhythm, and sound timing were extracted and evaluated using logistic regression. Patient information was included through a separate classifier. The model was further tested on the PhysioNet 2022 dataset to examine how well it performed on recordings from a different source. Evaluation on the BMD-HS dataset remained incomplete.

**Prediction and Evaluation:** The confidence scores produced by both main models were adjusted using a calibration process to make their predicted probabilities more reliable. We also explored whether the models could adapt to changes in input data without requiring additional labelled data or retraining. To test their reliability under difficult conditions, we introduced different types of disturbances, including background noise in heart recordings and blur, noise, compression, and reduced contrast in X-ray images. The predictions from the main models, additional medical features, and patient information were combined using a weighted voting approach. This process, called late fusion, allowed each component to contribute independently to the final result.

The combined heart and pneumonia probabilities were used to estimate the overall risk for a patient. The final referral decision was based on comparing the expected cost of missing a patient who needs medical attention with the cost of an unnecessary referral. This helped the system make a single screening recommendation for each visit.

**Deployment:** Finally, both deep learning models were converted into smaller 8-bit versions to reduce their storage and computational requirements, making them suitable for low-power devices. Their size, prediction speed, and agreement with the original models were evaluated. While the X-ray model met the size and speed requirements, its prediction agreement did not reach the intended 98% target. Overall, the methodology focused on developing a lightweight, offline screening system while separately evaluating the reliability, generalisation, and limitations of both pipelines.
